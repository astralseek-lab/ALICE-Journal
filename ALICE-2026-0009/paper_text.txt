Abstract
Background
Advancing and applying behavioural science requires clear, consistent
and coherent descriptions of behaviour change interventions.
Ontologies provide a means to do this and enable information to be
computer-readable. This study aimed to develop an ontology
describing the ‘schedule of delivery’ of behaviour change interventions
within the Behaviour Change Intervention Ontology (BCIO).
Methods
The Behaviour Change Intervention Schedule of Delivery Ontology (BCISDO)
was developed following the method developed for the BCIO: 1)
defining the ontology’s scope through literature review, 2) specifying
the entities, their labels, definitions and relationships, 3) refining the
ontology by piloting its use in annotations, 4) stakeholder review of
the ontology’s comprehensiveness and clarity; 5) inter-rater reliability
testing through annotating intervention reports using the ontology; 6)
specifying the relationships between classes; and 7) publishing a
machine-readable version of the ontology.

Conclusions
The BCISDO provides a reliable means of describing the temporal
organisation of behaviour change interventions. It is linked with other
BCIO ontologies describing characteristics of delivery (e.g. mode of
delivery), content and dose (i.e. the amount), contributing to the
accumulation of evidence in behavioural science.
Plain language summary
To improve interventions that aim to change behaviour, we need clear
and consistent ways to describe them. A method for doing this is by
using knowledge structures called ontologies. These organise
information, using clear definitions of terms and of relationships
between terms, for example the duration of an intervention or the
number or frequency of sessions over time. Every term is uniquely
defined which allows information organised in this way to be
computer readable. This study aimed to create an ontology to
describe how the timing of behaviour change interventions is
structured.
We developed the Behaviour Change Intervention Schedule of
Delivery Ontology by first reviewing the literature to define its scope.
The ontology focused on the duration, frequency, and timing of an
intervention. ‘Schedule of Delivery’ was defined as an attribute of
behaviour change interventions that refers to its temporal
organisation. From published research reports, we identified key
concepts (called classes), their names, definitions, and how they relate
each other. We tested and refined the ontology in several rounds,
asking experts to review it for clarity and completeness. The resulting
Schedule of Delivery Ontology consists of 49 classes. We published the
ontology in a computable-reader format, and freely available to all.
The Behaviour Change Intervention Schedule of Delivery Ontology
provides a reliable way to describe how behaviour change
interventions are organised over time. It connects with other parts of
the Behaviour Change Intervention Ontology, helping to build a

Behaviour change is essential to address many global issues
facing humanity today, including environmental, health, and
social crises. This creates a need for high quality research in
behaviour change (Michie et al., 2017). To achieve behaviour
change, it is necessary to understand the factors influencing
change, and to specify these factors precisely so that evaluations
of the processes and outcomes of interventions can by evalu-
ated, and knowledge about how best to bring about change can
be accumulated. Similarly, it is also important to precisely spec-
ify attributes of interventions, such as their content and dose,
and aspects of delivery, such as mode (e.g. digital, face-to-face;
Marques et al., 2021), and schedule (patterning over time). A
consistent, well-defined terminology and classification of the
schedule of a Behaviour Change Intervention (BCI; For the
definitions of technical terms used in this paper - in bold and
italicised-, see the glossary in Table 1) enable clearer descrip-
tions, more accurate replication and easier translation of effec-
tive interventions into practice (Michie et al., 2021). Further,
researchers can make more granular comparisons across large
numbers of studies to derive more specific conclusions, for
example to investigate how intervention content (Marques et al.,
2024; Michie et al., 2015) and aspects of delivery (Marques
et al., 2021; Norris et al., 2021b; Wright et al., 2023) interact
with other important aspects of interventions, such as the target
behaviour (Schenk et al., 2024a) and mechanisms of action
(Schenk et al., 2024b), over time.
The specification and reporting of behaviour change interven-
tions have improved over time, as frameworks have been devel-
oped. For example, the TIDIER checklist (Hoffmann et al.,
2014) includes 12 items, two of which refer to “how long”
interventions are and “when” they take place relative to other
important events (e.g. how long after diagnosis an intervention
is delivered). However, the TIDIER checklist does not describe,
classify or provide a consistent terminology for the schedule
of a BCI. The Behaviour Change Techniques Taxonomy v1
(BCTTv1) provided a more consistent terminology to specify
behaviour change techniques (BCTs) of interventions (Michie
et al., 2015), i.e., “the smallest parts of the content of a behav-
iour change intervention that are observable, replicable and on
their own have the potential to bring about behaviour change”
(Michie et al., 2021). Consisting of 93 BCTs, this taxonomy
has been widely used to select BCTs when developing inter-
ventions, more consistently report findings about BCTs, and
synthesise knowledge about BCTs (Corker et al., 2023). Frame-
works for representing knowledge, such as checklists and
taxonomies have been helpful but they lack the precision needed
to link domains of knowledge across complex topics and disci-
plines, or to ensure that the organised information is computer-
readable. Ontologies are structures for representing knowledge
that allow ‘interoperability’ and computer-readability.
Ontologies define classes of entities (anything that exists in
the universe) and their relations (Arp et al., 2015; Hastings,
2017). Entities and their relations are defined to represent their
essential properties in such a way that they are uniquely and
fully specified and assigned a unique label. This enables data
to be computer-readable, allowing computational analysis of
large amounts of complex data (Gene Ontology Consortium,
2015). As part of the Human Behaviour-Change Project (Michie
et al., 2017), researchers have developed ontologies for
representing behaviour change interventions - the Behaviour
Change Intervention Ontology (BCIO; Michie et al., 2021) and
its components.
The BCIO aims to represent all relevant aspects of behav-
ioural interventions and their evaluations. Therefore, classes
in the upper level of the ontology include the BCI scenario and
the BCI evaluation study. A BCI scenario is formally defined
as the “process in which a behaviour change intervention
is applied in a given context, including BCI engagement and
outcome behaviour” (Michie et al., 2021). Context refers to a
particular population in a particular setting. Within the ontol-
ogy, a BCI is specified through its content (i.e. behaviour
change technique, dose), and characterisis of the delivery of
the content (i.e. mode of delivery, source, style of delivery,
and schedule of delivery). Each of these characteristics are
formally specified through a hierarchy of classes of entities
elaborated in lower level ontologies. The BCIO component
ontologies include intervention setting (Norris et al., 2020),
target populations (ref), mode of delivery (Marques et al.,
2021), source of delivery (Norris et al., 2021b), style of deliv-
ery (Wright et al., 2023), behaviour change techniques (Marques
et al., 2024), mechanisms of action (Schenk et al., 2024b), and
human behaviour (Schenk et al., 2024a).
BCI content consists of specific techniques, such as set behav-
ioural goal BCT, which are delivered through various modes
of delivery (e.g. digital, in-person). Most interventions consist
of a range of such behaviour change techniques, which can be
delivered over shorter or longer time periods and at different
intervals (e.g., Schippers et al., 2017). Descriptions of the sched-
ule of BCI often focus on the length of time an intervention spans
(e.g. number/duration of sessions) and assume a straightfor-
ward relation to the dose, such that more intervention sessions
delivered for longer amounts of time are considered a larger
dose (Rowbotham et al., 2019). However, there are other
temporal aspects of the intervention schedule which may impact
on intervention effectiveness.
To be able to answer granular questions about when and how
much of a BCI or a BCT is necessary to achieve a desrired
behaviour change, we need greater conceptual clarity and to pro-
vide a means for researchers and interventionists to describe
temporal aspects of interventions in much detail as possible.
For example, we need to be able to clearly specify a BCT
delivered during six brief, five minute sessions across the
course of a two month intervention, and distinguish it from
the same BCT delivered for 30 minutes during a single ses-
sion. An ontology, with unambiguous classes that capture, label
and define such temporal aspects, could provide the language

principles/fp-000-summary.htmlAim
This study aimed to develop a clear, usable and reliable
ontology representing the BCI schedule of delivery, defined
as a “BCI attribute that is its temporal organisation”.
Methods
We developed the BCI Schedule Ontology in seven iterative
steps, broadly following the method for developing ontolo-
gies in the Human Behaviour-Change Project (Wright et al.,
2020).
Step 1- Defining the scope of the BCI Schedule of
Delivery Ontology through literature review
The first step involved a top-down scoping of the conceptual lit-
erature. Initially, our search was focused on aspects related to
the dose of interventions. A preliminary search was conducted in
Web of Science, Google Scholar, using different combination of
keywords, including: “dose”, “dosage”, “behavior”, “interven-
tion”, “review”, “framework”. Additionally, existing ontolo-
gies were searched on Ontology Lookup Service (Côté et al.,
2008).
The bottom-up approach involved annotating relevant infor-
mation from a set of BCI published reports that were part of a
large dataset of behaviour change intervention reports partially
annotated for behaviour change techniques, mechanisms of
action and modes of delivery, covering a range of health behav-
iours (Carey et al., 2019; Michie et al., 2015). Principles of
data saturation were followed with data coding conducted in
batches until no new information was found, resulting in the
annotation of 32 intervention reports.
Reviewing the initial list of entities from the literature review,
it was clear that two different dimensions were described as
part of dose: the quantity of content, and temporal arrange-
ment of delivery (e.g. frequency, duration). It was thus agreed
between the research team that conceptual clarity would be bet-
ter achieved by separating dose and schedule ontologies accord-
ingly (manuscript describing dose within the BCIO is under
preparation). A first definition of BCI schedule of delivery was
developed following principles of good ontological definitions
(Michie et al., 2019).
Step 2- Identifying key BCI Schedule of Delivery classes
and their definitions
The information extracted in Step 1 was mapped to create ini-
tial structures of the BCI Schedule of Delivery Ontology
by grouping similar entities as candidate classes, eliminat-
ing redundant terms, and excluding irrelevant terms. Entities
from other ontologies were directly reused if the ontology con-
formed with the Open Biological and Biomedical Ontology
(OBO) Foundry principles technical principles for ontologies

Step 3- Refinement of the BCI Schedule of Delivery
Ontology through an iterative process of literature
annotation, discussion and revision
We tested the first version of the BCI Schedule of Delivery
Ontology by annotating a small number of intervention reports
(10 on physical activity and 15 on smoking cessation) and made
some minor adjustments. After that, the ontology was used
to annotate a set of 80 smoking cessation behaviour change
intervention reports. Reports were randomised controlled tri-
als included in Cochrane reviews (List of papers used in devel-
opment of ontology: https://osf.io/4qcby/ (West et al., 2020)).
EPPI-Reviewer 4 software (Thomas et al., 2020) was used
for the annotations. An open alternative to this software used
for annotation is PDFAnno (Shindo et al., 2018). Annota-
tions followed an annotation guidance manual (West et al.,
2020). Two researchers (MM, CM & EH) independently anno-
tated the papers in batches of ~10, then met to discuss any dis-
crepancies and agree revisions to the codeset or to develop
further annotations guidance to improve the quality of annotations.
Throughout this process, changes to the ontology or guid-
ance were discussed with the research team of behavioural
science experts and an ontology expert. A revised version of the
BCI Schedule of Delivery Ontology was agreed which was
deemed stable and required no further annotation to generate
additional entities.
Step 4 – Expert stakeholder review
An international panel of behavioural scientists and pub-
lic health experts identified from UCL’s Centre for Behaviour
Change stakeholder list (Wright et al., 2020) were invited to
provide feedback on the the BCI Schedule of Delivery Ontol-
ogy resulting from Step 3. This list was made up of behaviour
change experts who: (i) provided feedback on previous stud-
ies in the UCL Centre for Behaviour Change and expressed
interest in being contacted for future studies, or (ii) registered
their interest in being contacted for stakeholder activities in the
Human Behaviour-Change Project. The random sample gen-
erator Research randomizer was used to select 100 participants
from the wider HBCP panel of 351 experts. Of the 100 experts,
72 were from “well-represented” countries in the stakeholder list
(the UK, USA, Canada, Netherlands and Australia), while 28
were from “less-well represented” countries (Germany, Estonia,
Japan, Chile, Uganda etc). Invitations to participate were sent via
email, and experts gave feedback by completing an online ques-
tionnaire delivered via Qualtrics software (https://osf.io/tm2fh)
(Norris et al., 2021a; West et al., 2020).
The task was designed to take no longer than 45 minutes to
complete. Experts were asked to: 1) Identify the characteris-
tics of an intervention schedule they would be most interested
in for understanding variation in the effectiveness of behaviour
change interventions (open-ended question); 2) Provide feed-
back on the completeness and comprehensiveness of the ontol-
ogy; 3) Identify any missing classes (if yes, which classes should
be added); 4) Identify any changes to classes or their defini-
tions (if yes, what changes should be considered); 5) indicate
if there were any classes that should be placed in a different
position within the ontology’s hierarchy.
Two researchers (AFM, MM) logged and responded to each stake-
holder comment in terms of the relevant change to the ontology
or the rationale for not further addressing the comment, after
which the larger research team provided feedback on the sug-
gested changes to the ontology. The BCI Schedule of Deliv-
ery Ontology and annotation guidance manual were revised in
accordance.
Step 5 – Inter-rater reliability of annotations using the
BCI Schedule of Delivery Ontology
To ensure that researchers could reliably annotate temporal
aspects of interventions, assessment of inter-rater reliability of
annotating intervention reports using the BCI Schedule of Deliv-
ery Ontology was done in two ways. First, two members of the
research team leading the development of this ontology (CM,
AFM), independently annotated references to the schedule of
delivery in 50 intervention reports (30 for smoking cessation
and 20 for physical activity), using EPPI-Reviewer. This number
of papers was selected as 50 papers would give a 10–15% mar-
gin of error around the estimated percentage agreement between
coders (Gwet, 2014; Wright et al., 2020). Secondly, two behav-
iour change experts unfamiliar with the ontology but with
MSc related to behavioural science and with experience in
annotating behaviour change intervention reports, annotated
another set of 50 reports with a variety of target behaviours
were randomly chosen using the EPPI Reviewer funtion, and
assigned to the experts using the distribute work functionality in
EPPI-Reviewer, from a database of papers. Annotations fol-
lowed an annotation guidance manual (https://osf.io/paem8)
(West et al., 2020).
Annotated data was extracted as JSON files from EPPI-Reviewer
and run through the Automation Inter-Rater Reliability script
for calculating inter-rater reliability, developed as part of the
Human
Behaviour
Change
Project
(Finnerty
&
Moore,
2020), incorporating the Python script Krippendorff 0.3.2.
Krippendorff’s alpha (Hayes & Krippendorff, 2007) was used
as the inter-rater reliability coefficient as it corrects well for
chance and can be used for multiple data-types (e.g. nominal,
ordinal or interval).
Additional changes to the BCI Schedule of Delivery Ontology
were made based on the issues arising from inter-rater reliabil-
ity testing, and final revision of classes, definitions and labels
by the research team.Step 6 - Specifying relationships between classes
The research team specified and refined the relationships
between the classes in the BCI Schedule of Delivery Ontol-
ogy, making sure all relationships were in line with BFO and
the Relation Ontology (Smith et al., 2005). For example, the
hierarchical “is_a” relationship is used to connect subclasses
of intervention schedule of delivery (e.g, a regular sched-
ule of delivery can be specified as a subclass of intervention
schedule of delivery).
Step 7 – Making the Schedule of Delivery Ontology
machine-readable and available online
The BCI Schedule of Delivery Ontology was represented in tables
including classes’ labels, definitions, elaborations, key relation-
ships, examples and synonyms. To release the ontology in a
machine readable and interoperable format, we created a ver-
sion in Web Ontology Language (OWL) format (Antoniou &
Van Harmelen, 2004), enabling it to be searched, visualised
and queried, using standard ontology tools and software. A
script based on the ROBOT ontology toolkit library (Jackson
et al., 2019) was used to convert the primary Excel files in
which ontology developers specify content into the OWL for-
mat, linking it with the upper level of the BCIO, incorporating
relevant metadata such as labels, synonyms and definitions, and
the relationships between classes. The OWL version of the BCI
Schedule of Delivery Ontology is stored in the Human Behav-
iour-Change Project’s GitHub repository, an online platform for
sharing and versioning resources.
The GitHub repository has an issue tracker which allows feed-
back and queries to be submitted by members of the GitHub
community; these can be responded to and, if necessary,
addressed in subsequent ontology releases. The BCI Schedule
of Delivery is part of the Behaviour Change Intervention Ontol-
ogy which is available online in the Behavioural and Social
Sciences Ontology Foundry, a repository for ontologies in
the behavioural and social science domains; and an associ-
ated community of practice is being built. The final Behaviour
Change Intervention Ontology will also be submitted to the
Open Biological and Biomedical Ontology (OBO) Foundry
(Smith et al., 2007).
Results
Step 1- Defining the scope of the BCI Schedule of
Delivery Ontology through literature review
The conceptual literature research presented information about
ways of operationalising the schedule of delivery of a BCI. The
main features of schedule found in the 32 reports were dura-
tion (68%), frequency (59%) and schedule (34%). The reports
also contained many examples of measures of time (e.g. min-
utes, hours, weeks), frequency (e.g. weekly, monthly) and rela-
tive time (e.g. five weeks after initiation of support). Schedule
was initially defined as “The temporal organisation of a behav-
iour change intervention, expressed in terms of the overall
duration of the intervention, the number of components and
their duration, frequency and regularity.”
Step 2- Identifying key BCI Schedule of Delivery classes
and their definitions
The first version of the Schedule Ontology contained 9 classes
(available at: https://osf.io/qmcf4/) (West et al., 2020), which
encompassed the duration or temporal organisation of events,
such as intervention contacts (e.g. number, duration or fre-
quency of sessions). Additional temporal classes (e.g. when
an intervention session takes place relative to other events),
were also included.
Step 3- Refinement of the BCI Schedule of Delivery
Ontology through an iterative process of literature
annotation and discussion
Annotations of 105 intervention reports using the BCI Sched-
ule of Delivery Ontology resulted in revisions. The definition
of Schedule of Delivery was redefined as ‘An attribute of a BCI

Step 4 – Expert stakeholder review
From the 100 experts invited to participate in the stakeholder
review task, 28 experts responded to the request but only 17
of those that responded completed the task; 24% of the com-
pleted responses were from experts from “less-well repre-
sented” countries. Their feedback was analysed and responses
to the suggested changes can be found at (https://osf.io/z98tq)
(West et al., 2020).
No additional classes were added to or removed from the ontol-
ogy at this step; however, the ontology was updated to give
more informal definitions and elaborations to make the classes
clearer as requested by the experts. The annotation guidance
manual was also refined based on expert uncertainties of how
to use several of the classes (e.g., being able to apply a class to
an intervention aspect more than once) and whether the classes
related to the planned or actual events in the interven-
tion (the classes relate to the intervention scenario which is
what was reported rather than planned for the intervention).
Step 5 – Inter-rater reliability of annotations using the
BCI Schedule of Delivery Ontology
Krippendorff’s alpha values were calculated from 50 papers
reporting smoking cessation and reporting physical activity
behavior change interventions that were annotated by research-
ers familiar with the schedule ontology. A value of a=0.54
was obtained, which is lower than what is deemed acceptable
(a=0.60). Interval temporal part and associated classes were the
ones that revealed more difficulties with annotation, and thedescription of these classes was improved in the manual guid-
ance for the external annotation. Another 50 papers reporting
interventions in the same behavioral domain that were annotated
by behavioural science researchers unfamiliar with the ontolo-
gies generated a alpha value of a=0.60, indicating an acceptable
reliability.
Discussions between the research team on consistency between
classes, and labels and defintions led to final revisions of
the ontology. The following changes were made:
•
Revision of the definition of BCI Schedule of Delivery
to: "A behaviour change intervention attribute that is
its temporal organisation".
•
Revision of the labels of all entities to "BCI" instead
of intervention
•
Inclusion of two additional classes: BCI Duration
(“<A temporal interval> between the star and end of
a BCI”), and order of BCI temporal parts (“a <BCI
attribute> that is the temporal positioning of BCI
parts.
•
Data items that were described as "aggregates" (capturing
data relating to the minimum, maximum, mean or
median for temporal classes, such as BCI duration)
were moved to separate classes, presenting a "is about"
relation with their parent class.
Step 6 - Specifying relationships between classes
A key relationship specified between classed and their parent
classes was the “is_a” relationship, for example, “regular BCI
schedule of delivery” is_a “BCI schedule of delivery”. Another
relationship that associates classes that are representational or
quantitative in nature, such as data iems, to the relevant class that
they are about was the “is_about” relationship. For example,
the following relationship links “mean BCI duration statistic”
to “BCI duration”: “mean BCI duration statistic” is_about “BCI
duration”. In addition, the “is_attribute_of” relationship ena-
bled us to specify that “BCI schedule of delivery” is an attribute
or a characteristic of a “behaviour change intervention”: “BCI
schedule of delivery” is_attribute_of “behaviour change inter-
vention. Finally, the parthood relationship (“part_of”) allowed us
to capture composition. For example, an intervention temporal
part is a part of an intervention.
Step 7 – Making the Schedule of Delivery Ontology
machine-readable and available online
Classes IDs, labels, definitions, parent classes and type of rela-
tions are presented in Table 2. The final version of the BCI
Schedule of Delivery Ontology contains 49 classes. Of these,
14 classes specify unique entities related to the duration and
frequency of a BCI, its temporal parts and contact events, and
35 are associated aggregate data items (statistic, mean, median,
minimum, maximum).
The BCI Schedule of Delivery Ontology is available in a
downloadable format available from GitHub (https://github.com/
HumanBehaviourChangeProject/ontologies/blob/master/Sched-
ule/bcio_schedule.owl), and it can be browsed in the dedicated
BCIO Search Tool and Ontology Lookup Service. The ontol-
ogy is accompanied by an annotation guidance manual that pro-
vides guidance on how to annotate for these entities in BCI
reports (https://osf.io/2rfwy). (West et al., 2020).
Discussion
This study developed a formal and machine-readable specifica-
tion and classification of the characteristics of the schedule of
an intervention (e.g. duration) - the Behaviour Change Inter-
vention Schedule of Delivery Ontology, as part of the Behav-
iour Change Intervention Ontology (Michie et al., 2021).
It consists of 49 classes, of which 14 specified entities related
to the duration and frequency of a BCI, its temporal parts and
contact events, and 35 were associated aggregate data items (sta-
tistic, mean, median, minimum, maximum). This method of
specifying characteristics of the schedule of delivery of behav-
iour change interventions in terms of classes, labels and defini-
tions provides a shared vocabulary for reporting and describing
interventions, improving clarity, reducing ambiguity and enabling
communication across disciplines.
While the preliminary development focused on the concept of
dose, the initial literature research and annotations, and discus-
sion within the research team, revealed that within the domain
of behavioural science, most of the concepts named as dose
characteristics were characteristics of schedule, i.e. temporal
organisation of the delivery of an intervention, and aspects related
with duration and frequency of an intervention and its parts.
Dose is exclusively the amount of content, which may refer to
the number of behaviour change techniques that are used and is
further described elsewhere. Schedule is related to the delivery
of the content of interventions, i.e. their timing. The distinction
between these entities and their representation through an ontol-
ogy is crucial to improve reporting, accuracy of evidence-
synthesis and understanding. The annotations done during
the development of this ontology revealed the complexi-
ties of annotating unique entities of time and parts in existing
intervention reports, due to the poor reporting of these char-
acteristics. In many instances there was no detailed infor-
mation about the schedule of the different parts of an
intervention. Lack of detail in describing the temporal char-
acteristics of BCIs shows the need for the formalisation of
knowledge related to the schedule of delivery of BCIs.
Strengths and limitations
A strength of this work is the fact that the ontologies are inter-
operable (i.e. are interlinked and can be used together) with
other ontologies within the BCIO developed within the Human
Behaviour-Change Project, including delivery characteristics
such as the Mode of Delivery Ontology (Marques et al., 2021),
Intervention Source Ontology (Norris et al., 2021a), and
Style of Delivery (Wright et al., 2023), and ontologies that
represent the content of BCIs - Behaviour Change Techniques
Ontology (Marques et al., 2024) and Dose Ontology. Because
ontologies are machine-readable, they support complex, rapid
analysis of large datasets to produce, for example, advanced
evidence syntheses and predictions of intervention outcomes.

A strength of the methodology used was the inclusion of
an expert panel consultation from a variety of domains and
backgrounds who appraised and revised the ontology (Norris
et al., 2021a; Wright et al., 2020), ensuring that the classes
are as comprehensive as possible and provide good coverage
of the phenomena for its purposes.
A limitation of this work is that the dataset of report papers used
for the annotations of the schedule classes only covered someof the types of behaviour targeted by behaviour change inter-
ventions (e.g., smoking cessation and physical activity for the
inter-rater reliability testing). It is likely that future application
of the ontology for other behaviours and contexts may identify
additional classes that can be added to this ontology. The onto-
logical representations of schedule will need to be updated
according to advances in behavioral science and based on the
feedback from users that can be submitted through GitHub. Users
may wish to add new classes or relations, or suggest changes to
labels or definitions. This can be done by creating an “Issue”
on the GitHub portal for the BCIO (https://github.com/Human-
BehaviourChangeProject/ontologies/issues). Ontology users can
submit suggested changes about the ontology (e.g., adding
or changing classes) through the GitHub Issues Tracker
(https://github.com/ontology-tools/onto-spread-ed/issues).
To submit changes, people first need to create an account on
Github, and then submit an issue by clicking “New Issue” on
the provided link for the issue tracker. Any submitted issue will
be monitored, responded to and addressed by changing the
ontology or providing guidance where relevant.
Parts of the Behaviour Change Intervention Schedule of
Delivery Ontology have been used to inform the development
of classes for the temporal patterning of behaviours, such as
“behavioural duration” (Schenk et al., 2024a); these classes will
be further developed and reported in a forthcoming paper in
Wellcome Open Research.
Conclusion
The Behaviour Change Intervention (BCI) Schedule of Deliv-
ery Ontology constitutes a shared vocabulary describing char-
acteristics of the temporal organisation of behaviour change
interventions. It can be used to support intervention reporting,
descriptions and intervention development about the tempo-
ral aspects of behaviour change interventions. Moreover, by
incorporating feedback from its users into the Schedule Ontol-
ogy in the future, the ontology will become increasingly
useful for applications. The Schedule Ontology can also
be linked and used with other parts of the BCIO describing
characteristics of delivery (e.g. mode of delivery), content
(behaviour change techniques) and dose (i.e. the amount), con-
tributing to the detailed and precise accumulation of evidence
in behavioural science.
